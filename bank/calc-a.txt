@ calc e calc-e-013

@ calc e calc-e-014
T: One-sided limits of |x|/x
S: limits
Q: What is $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\frac{|x|}{x}$?
A: $1$
B: $-1$
C: $0$
D: does not exist
ANS: D
EXP: For $x>0$, $\tfrac{|x|}{x}=1$, so the right-hand limit is $1$. For $x<0$, $\tfrac{|x|}{x}=-1$, so the left-hand limit is $-1$.
A two-sided limit exists only when both one-sided limits exist and are equal. Here $1\ne-1$, so it does not exist.
Answering $1$ only checks the right side.

@ calc e calc-e-015
T: Continuity and differentiability of |x|
S: continuity-differentiability
Q: At $x=0$, the function $f(x)=|x|$ is:
A: continuous and differentiable
B: continuous but not differentiable
C: differentiable but not continuous
D: neither continuous nor differentiable
ANS: B
EXP: Continuity: $\lim_{x\to0}|x|=0=f(0)$.
Differentiability: the right derivative is $\lim_{h\to0^+}\tfrac{|h|}{h}=1$ and the left derivative is $-1$. They differ, so $f'(0)$ does not exist (a corner).
"Differentiable but not continuous" is impossible: differentiability always implies continuity. This kink is why the L1 penalty in lasso needs subgradients.

@ calc m calc-m-022
T: Smoothness of the Huber loss
S: continuity-differentiability
Q: The Huber loss with $\delta=1$ is $h(r)=\tfrac12r^2$ for $|r|\le1$ and $h(r)=|r|-\tfrac12$ for $|r|>1$. Which statement is true?
A: $h$ is not continuous at $r=\pm1$
B: $h$ is continuous but not differentiable at $r=\pm1$
C: $h$ is differentiable everywhere, but $h''$ does not exist at $r=\pm1$
D: $h$ is twice differentiable everywhere
ANS: C
EXP: At $r=1$: values $\tfrac12$ and $1-\tfrac12=\tfrac12$ agree, so $h$ is continuous.

@ calc m calc-m-023

@ calc m calc-m-028

ANS: 0.80

Again: $g''(x)=\varphi(x)+\varphi(x)+x\varphi'(x)=2\varphi(x)-x^2\varphi(x)$ (using $\varphi'(x)=-x\varphi(x)$).
$g''(0)=2\varphi(0)=\dfrac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi}}=\sqrt{\tfrac2\pi}\approx0.80$.
Forgetting the second $\varphi$ from the product rule gives $\varphi(0)\approx0.40$. Unlike ReLU, whose second derivative is $0$ away from $0$ and undefined at $0$, GELU is smoothly curved at the origin.

@ calc h calc-h-030
T: True facts about derivatives
S: derivatives
TYPE: msq
Q: Let $f$ and $g$ be differentiable on an open interval $I$. Which statements are always true? (More than one option may be correct.)
A: If $f'(x)>0$ for all $x\in I$, then $f$ is strictly increasing on $I$.
B: If $f$ is strictly increasing on $I$, then $f'(x)>0$ for all $x\in I$.
C: If $f'(x)=g'(x)$ for all $x\in I$, then $f-g$ is constant on $I$.
D: If $f'(c)=0$ for some $c\in I$, then $f$ has a local extremum at $c$.
ANS: A,C
EXP: [[A]]: by the mean value theorem, $f(b)-f(a)=f'(\xi)(b-a)>0$ for $a<b$. True.
[[B]]: $f(x)=x^3$ is strictly increasing but $f'(0)=0$. False.
[[C]]: $(f-g)'=0$ on an interval, so by the MVT $f-g$ is constant. True. (On a union of disjoint intervals it could be a different constant on each piece.)
[[D]]: $x^3$ again: $f'(0)=0$ but $0$ is not an extremum. False.

@ calc h calc-h-031
T: Tangent to ln x through the origin
S: derivatives
Q: A line through the origin is tangent to $y=\ln x$. What is its slope?
A: $1$
B: $e$
C: $\frac1e$
D: $\frac12$
ANS: C
EXP: Let the tangency point be $(a,\ln a)$. The tangent slope is $\tfrac1a$, and a line through the origin and $(a,\ln a)$ has slope $\tfrac{\ln a}{a}$.
Equate: $\tfrac1a=\tfrac{\ln a}{a}\Rightarrow\ln a=1\Rightarrow a=e$. Slope $=\tfrac1e$.
$e$ is the $x$-coordinate of the tangency point, not the slope; $1$ is the slope of $\ln x$ at $x=1$, where the tangent $y=x-1$ misses the origin.

@ calc h calc-h-032
T: Tenth derivative of 1/(1+x²) at zero
S: taylor-series
TYPE: nat
TOL: 0
Q: Let $f(x)=\dfrac{1}{1+x^2}$. Find $f^{(10)}(0)$.
ANS: -3628800
EXP: Geometric series with ratio $-x^2$: $\dfrac1{1+x^2}=\sum_{k\ge0}(-1)^kx^{2k}$ for $|x|<1$.
The $x^{10}$ term has $k=5$, coefficient $(-1)^5=-1$.
Since this coefficient equals $\dfrac{f^{(10)}(0)}{10!}$: $f^{(10)}(0)=-10!=-3628800$.
Differentiating ten times directly is hopeless; reading coefficients off a known series is the key insight. Forgetting the sign gives $+10!$.

@ calc h calc-h-033
T: Smooth but not analytic
S: taylor-series
TYPE: msq
Q: Let $f(x)=e^{-1/x^2}$ for $x\ne0$ and $f(0)=0$. Which statements are true? (More than one option may be correct.)
A: $f$ is infinitely differentiable at $0$.
B: $f^{(n)}(0)=0$ for every $n\ge0$.
C: The Maclaurin series of $f$ converges to $f(x)$ for every $x$.
D: $f$ has a local maximum at $x=0$.
ANS: A,B
EXP: Every derivative of $f$ for $x\ne0$ has the form $p(1/x)\,e^{-1/x^2}$ with $p$ a polynomial, and $e^{-1/x^2}$ goes to $0$ faster than any power of $1/x$ blows up. So each difference quotient at $0$ tends to $0$: [[A]] and [[B]] are true.

[[D]]: $f(0)=0<f(x)$ for $x\ne0$, so $0$ is a minimum. False.

@ calc h calc-h-034
T: Second-order estimate of softplus
S: taylor-series
TYPE: nat
TOL: 0.002

@ calc h calc-h-035
T: Radius of convergence of 1/(1+x²)
S: taylor-series
Q: The function $\dfrac1{1+x^2}$ is infinitely differentiable on all of $\mathbb R$. What is the radius of convergence of its Maclaurin series?
A: $1$
B: $\infty$
C: $2$
D: $\frac12$
ANS: A
EXP: The series is $\sum(-1)^kx^{2k}$, a geometric series in $-x^2$, which converges iff $x^2<1$, i.e. $|x|<1$. So $R=1$.
The reason, despite no trouble on the real line: in the complex plane $1+z^2=0$ at $z=\pm i$, at distance $1$ from $0$, and the radius of convergence is the distance to the nearest singularity.
Answering $\infty$ assumes that smooth on $\mathbb R$ means the series converges everywhere.

@ calc h calc-h-036
T: Recognising the alternating harmonic series
S: taylor-series
Q: $1-\dfrac12+\dfrac13-\dfrac14+\cdots=$
A: $1$
B: $\ln2$
C: $\dfrac\pi4$
D: $\dfrac1e$
ANS: B
EXP: $\ln(1+x)=x-\dfrac{x^2}2+\dfrac{x^3}3-\cdots$ for $-1<x\le1$. At $x=1$ (allowed, since the alternating series converges and Abel's theorem applies), the sum is $\ln2\approx0.693$.

@ calc h calc-h-037
T: How the Taylor error scales with step size
S: taylor-series
Q: Near $x=0$, approximate $e^x$ by $1+x+\dfrac{x^2}2$. If $x$ is halved (from a small value), the error is multiplied by approximately:
A: $\frac12$
B: $\frac14$
C: $\frac1{16}$
D: $\frac18$
ANS: D
EXP: The error is the first omitted term: $e^x-\left(1+x+\tfrac{x^2}2\right)=\dfrac{x^3}{6}+O(x^4)$.
It scales as $x^3$, so halving $x$ multiplies it by $\left(\tfrac12\right)^3=\tfrac18$.
$\tfrac14$ uses the order of the last kept term ($x^2$) instead of the first omitted one. The same reasoning explains why central differences ($O(h^2)$ error) beat forward differences ($O(h)$) when checking gradients numerically.

@ calc h calc-h-038
T: Checking third-order expansions
S: taylor-series
TYPE: msq
Q: Which of the following Maclaurin expansions are correct up to and including the $x^3$ term? (More than one option may be correct.)
A: $\tan x=x+\dfrac{x^3}{3}+\cdots$
B: $\sqrt{1+x}=1+\dfrac x2-\dfrac{x^2}8+\dfrac{x^3}{16}+\cdots$
C: $e^x\sin x=x+x^2+\dfrac{x^3}{2}+\cdots$

@ calc h calc-h-039
T: When the second derivative test fails
S: maxima-minima
Q: Let $f(x)=e^x+e^{-x}-2-x^2$. At $x=0$, $f'(0)=f''(0)=0$. What does $f$ have at $x=0$?
A: A local maximum
B: An inflection point
C: A local minimum
D: Neither an extremum nor an inflection point
ANS: C
EXP: The second derivative test is inconclusive, so go to higher order. $e^x+e^{-x}=2\cosh x=2+x^2+\dfrac{x^4}{12}+\cdots$.
So $f(x)=\dfrac{x^4}{12}+\dfrac{x^6}{360}+\cdots>0=f(0)$ for $x\ne0$: a local (in fact global) minimum.
Higher-order test: the first nonzero derivative is $f^{(4)}(0)=2>0$ and of even order, so it is a minimum. If the first nonzero derivative had odd order, it would be an inflection point, which is the tempting but wrong conclusion here.

@ calc h calc-h-040
T: Three real roots of x³ − 3x + k
S: maxima-minima
Q: For which values of $k$ does $x^3-3x+k=0$ have three distinct real roots?
A: $|k|<2$
B: $|k|\le2$
C: $|k|<1$
D: $k>2$
ANS: A
EXP: Let $f(x)=x^3-3x+k$. $f'(x)=3x^2-3=0\Rightarrow x=\pm1$: local max $f(-1)=k+2$, local min $f(1)=k-2$.
A cubic has three distinct real roots iff its local max is above $0$ and local min is below $0$: $k+2>0$ and $k-2<0$, so $-2<k<2$.
At $k=\pm2$ the graph touches the axis at an extremum, giving a repeated root, so only two distinct roots; that is why $|k|\le2$ is wrong.

@ calc h calc-h-041
T: Global max of x²e^(−x) on [0, ∞)
S: maxima-minima
Q: What is the global maximum value of $f(x)=x^2e^{-x}$ on $[0,\infty)$?
A: $\dfrac1e$
B: $\dfrac4{e^2}$
C: $\dfrac2e$
D: $\dfrac4e$
ANS: B
EXP: $f'(x)=(2x-x^2)e^{-x}=x(2-x)e^{-x}$: zero at $x=0$ and $x=2$.
$f'>0$ on $(0,2)$ and $f'<0$ for $x>2$, so $x=2$ is a max. Endpoint $f(0)=0$ (the minimum) and $f\to0$ as $x\to\infty$.

@ calc h calc-h-042
T: Inflection points of the Gaussian bell
S: maxima-minima
Q: The function $\varphi(x)=\exp\!\left(-\dfrac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)$ (with $\sigma>0$) has its inflection points at:
A: $x=\mu$
B: $x=\mu\pm\dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt2}$
C: $x=\mu\pm\sigma$
D: $x=\mu\pm2\sigma$
ANS: C
EXP: Let $u=\tfrac{x-\mu}{\sigma}$. Then $\varphi'=-\tfrac{u}{\sigma}\varphi$ and $\varphi''=\tfrac{1}{\sigma^2}(u^2-1)\varphi$.
$\varphi''$ changes sign where $u^2=1$, i.e. $x=\mu\pm\sigma$. Between them the bell is concave, outside it is convex.
$x=\mu$ is the maximum, not an inflection point. $\mu\pm\tfrac\sigma{\sqrt2}$ is correct only for $e^{-(x-\mu)^2/\sigma^2}$, which lacks the factor $2$. This is how $\sigma$ can be read off a plotted normal curve.

@ calc h calc-h-043

@ calc h calc-h-044
T: Which is larger, e^π or π^e
S: maxima-minima
Q: By studying $f(x)=\dfrac{\ln x}{x}$, decide which is true.

B: $\pi^e>e^\pi$

D: It cannot be decided without a calculator
ANS: A
