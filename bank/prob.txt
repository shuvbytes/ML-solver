@ prob e prob-e-001
T: Sum of two dice equals 7
S: counting
Q: Two fair dice are rolled. What is the probability that the sum is 7?
A: $\frac16$
B: $\frac{1}{12}$
C: $\frac{7}{36}$
D: $\frac19$
ANS: A
EXP: Favourable outcomes: $(1,6),(2,5),(3,4),(4,3),(5,2),(6,1)$, which is 6 of 36. So $P=\tfrac{6}{36}=\tfrac16$. Seven is the most likely sum.


@ prob e prob-e-002
T: Probability of a union
S: axioms-events
Q: $P(A)=0.5$, $P(B)=0.4$, $P(A\cap B)=0.2$. Find $P(A\cup B)$.
A: $0.9$
B: $0.7$
C: $0.5$
D: $0.2$
ANS: B
EXP: Inclusion–exclusion: $P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)=0.5+0.4-0.2=0.7$.
$0.9$ double-counts the overlap.


@ prob e prob-e-003
T: Union of independent events
S: independence
Q: $A$ and $B$ are independent with $P(A)=0.3$ and $P(B)=0.5$. Find $P(A\cup B)$.
A: $0.8$
B: $0.65$
C: $0.15$
D: $0.5$
ANS: B
EXP: Independence gives $P(A\cap B)=0.3\times0.5=0.15$.
Then $P(A\cup B)=0.3+0.5-0.15=0.65$. Equivalently, $1-P(A^c)P(B^c)=1-0.7\cdot0.5=0.65$.

@ prob e prob-e-007
T: Maximum variance of a Bernoulli
S: discrete-dists
Q: What is the largest possible variance of a Bernoulli$(p)$ random variable?
A: $0.5$
B: $1$
C: $0.25$
D: $0.125$
ANS: C
EXP: $\operatorname{Var}=p(1-p)$, a downward parabola maximised at $p=\tfrac12$: $\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12=0.25$.
That is why the Gini impurity and entropy of a binary node are highest at a 50/50 split.


@ prob e prob-e-008
T: Mutually exclusive vs independent
S: independence
Q: $A$ and $B$ are mutually exclusive with $P(A)>0$ and $P(B)>0$. Then $A$ and $B$ are:
A: always independent
B: never independent
C: independent only if $P(A)=P(B)$
D: independent only if $P(A)+P(B)=1$
ANS: B
EXP: Mutually exclusive means $P(A\cap B)=0$. Independence needs $P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)>0$. These cannot both hold.
Intuitively, knowing $A$ happened tells you $B$ did not, which is strong dependence.

@ prob m prob-m-001
T: Bayes’ theorem for a medical test
S: conditional-bayes
Q: A disease affects 1% of people. A test detects it 99% of the time and gives a false positive 5% of the time. A random person tests positive. Approximately what is $P(\text{disease}\mid +)$?
A: $\approx0.99$
B: $\approx0.17$
C: $\approx0.05$
D: $\approx0.50$
ANS: B
EXP: $P(+)=0.01\cdot0.99+0.99\cdot0.05=0.0099+0.0495=0.0594$.
$P(D\mid+)=\dfrac{0.0099}{0.0594}=\dfrac16\approx0.167$.
The low base rate dominates: most positives come from the large healthy group (base-rate fallacy).

@ prob m prob-m-004
T: Memoryless property of the exponential
S: continuous-dists
Q: $X\sim\text{Exp}(\lambda=0.5)$. Find $P(X>5\mid X>2)$.
A: $e^{-1.5}$
B: $e^{-2.5}$
C: $e^{-1}$
D: $1-e^{-1.5}$
ANS: A
EXP: Memorylessness: $P(X>s+t\mid X>s)=P(X>t)$. With $t=3$: $P(X>3)=e^{-0.5\cdot3}=e^{-1.5}$.
Directly: $\dfrac{e^{-2.5}}{e^{-1}}=e^{-1.5}$.


@ prob m prob-m-005
T: Expected maximum of two dice
S: random-variables
Q: Two fair dice are rolled. What is $E[\max(X_1,X_2)]$?
A: $\frac{161}{36}$
B: $\frac72$
C: $4$
D: $\frac{91}{36}$
ANS: A
EXP: $P(\max\le k)=(k/6)^2$, so $P(\max\ge k)=1-\tfrac{(k-1)^2}{36}$.
$E[M]=\sum_{k=1}^{6}P(M\ge k)=6-\tfrac{0+1+4+9+16+25}{36}=6-\tfrac{55}{36}=\tfrac{161}{36}\approx4.47$.
$\tfrac{91}{36}$ is $E[\min]$; the two add to $7=E[X_1+X_2]$.


@ prob m prob-m-006
T: Correlation from variances
S: covariance-correlation
Q: $\operatorname{Var}(X)=4$, $\operatorname{Var}(Y)=9$ and $\operatorname{Var}(X+Y)=10$. What is the correlation $\rho(X,Y)$?
A: $-0.25$
B: $-1.5$
C: $0.25$
D: $-0.5$
ANS: A
EXP: $\operatorname{Var}(X+Y)=\operatorname{Var}X+\operatorname{Var}Y+2\operatorname{Cov}\Rightarrow10=13+2\operatorname{Cov}\Rightarrow\operatorname{Cov}=-1.5$.
$\rho=\dfrac{-1.5}{2\cdot3}=-0.25$. ($-1.5$ is the covariance, not the correlation.)

@ prob m prob-m-009
T: Race between two exponentials
S: joint-conditional
Q: $X\sim\text{Exp}(1)$ and $Y\sim\text{Exp}(2)$ are independent. Find $P(X<Y)$.
A: $\frac13$
B: $\frac23$
C: $\frac12$
D: $\frac14$
ANS: A
EXP: $P(X<Y)=\dfrac{\lambda_X}{\lambda_X+\lambda_Y}=\dfrac{1}{1+2}=\dfrac13$.
$Y$ has the higher rate, so it usually happens first. Derivation: $\int_0^\infty e^{-x}e^{-2x}\,dx=\tfrac13$.


@ prob m prob-m-010
T: Zero covariance does not imply independence
S: covariance-correlation
Q: $X\sim U(-1,1)$ and $Y=X^2$. Which is true?
A: $\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=0$ and they are independent
B: $\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=0$ but they are dependent
C: $\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)>0$
D: $\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)<0$
ANS: B
EXP: $\operatorname{Cov}(X,X^2)=E[X^3]-E[X]E[X^2]=0-0=0$ by symmetry.
Yet $Y$ is a deterministic function of $X$, so they are clearly dependent. Covariance only measures linear association.


@ prob h prob-h-001
T: Coupon collector with 4 coupons
S: expectation-variance
Q: Each cereal box contains one of 4 equally likely coupons. What is the expected number of boxes needed to collect all 4?
A: $\frac{25}{3}$
B: $4$
C: $10$
D: $\frac{16}{3}$
ANS: A
EXP: After collecting $i$ distinct coupons, the wait for a new one is Geometric with $p=\tfrac{4-i}{4}$.
$E=\tfrac44+\tfrac43+\tfrac42+\tfrac41=4\left(1+\tfrac12+\tfrac13+\tfrac14\right)=4\cdot\tfrac{25}{12}=\tfrac{25}{3}\approx8.33$.
In general $E=nH_n\approx n\ln n$.


@ prob h prob-h-002
T: Waiting for two consecutive sixes
S: conditional-expectation
Q: A fair die is rolled until two 6s appear in a row. What is the expected number of rolls?
A: $36$
B: $12$
C: $42$
D: $30$
ANS: C
EXP: Let $E_0$ = expected rolls from scratch, $E_1$ = expected rolls when the last roll was a 6.
$E_1=1+\tfrac56E_0$ and $E_0=1+\tfrac16E_1+\tfrac56E_0$.
Substituting: $E_0=\tfrac76+\tfrac{35}{36}E_0\Rightarrow\tfrac1{36}E_0=\tfrac76\Rightarrow E_0=42$.

@ prob h prob-h-004
T: Monty Hall with four doors
S: conditional-bayes
Q: There are 4 doors and 1 car. You pick a door; the host, who knows where the car is, opens one other empty door. You then switch to one of the 2 remaining unopened doors chosen at random. $P(\text{win})$?
A: $\frac14$
B: $\frac38$
C: $\frac12$
D: $\frac34$
ANS: B
EXP: Your first pick is wrong with probability $\tfrac34$; then the car is behind one of the 2 other unopened doors and you pick it with probability $\tfrac12$.
If your first pick was right ($\tfrac14$), switching always loses. So $P=\tfrac34\cdot\tfrac12=\tfrac38>\tfrac14$ for staying.

@ prob h prob-h-006
T: Reading variance from an MGF
S: expectation-variance
Q: The moment generating function of $X$ is $M_X(t)=e^{2t+8t^2}$. What is $\operatorname{Var}(X)$?
A: $8$
B: $16$
C: $4$
D: $2$
ANS: B

@ prob h prob-h-008

@ prob h prob-h-009
T: Stationary distribution of a 2-state Markov chain
S: markov-chains
Q: A Markov chain has transition matrix $P=\begin{pmatrix}0.9&0.1\\0.5&0.5\end{pmatrix}$ (rows = current state). What is its stationary distribution $\pi$?
A: $\left(\frac56,\frac16\right)$
B: $\left(\frac12,\frac12\right)$
C: $(0.9,\ 0.1)$
D: $\left(\frac16,\frac56\right)$
ANS: A
EXP: Solve $\pi P=\pi$. Balance of flow between the states: $\pi_1\cdot0.1=\pi_2\cdot0.5\Rightarrow\pi_1=5\pi_2$.
With $\pi_1+\pi_2=1$: $\pi=(\tfrac56,\tfrac16)$.


@ prob h prob-h-010
T: Chebyshev’s inequality
S: limit-theorems
Q: $X$ has mean 50 and standard deviation 5 (distribution unknown). The best upper bound Chebyshev gives for $P(|X-50|\ge15)$ is:
A: $\frac13$
B: $\frac19$
C: $\frac14$
D: $0.003$
ANS: B
EXP: Chebyshev: $P(|X-\mu|\ge k\sigma)\le\tfrac1{k^2}$. Here $15=3\sigma$, so the bound is $\tfrac19$.
$0.003$ is the normal-distribution value, which you cannot assume when the distribution is unknown.
