@ la e la-e-001
T: Rank of a 2×2 matrix
S: rank-nullity
Q: What is the rank of $A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&4\end{pmatrix}$?
A: $0$
B: $1$
C: $2$
D: $4$
ANS: B
EXP: Row 2 is exactly $2\times$ Row 1, so there is only one linearly independent row.
Check: $\det A = 1\cdot4-2\cdot2=0$, so rank $<2$; and $A\neq 0$, so rank $\ge 1$. Hence rank $=1$.


@ la e la-e-002
T: Eigenvalues of a triangular matrix
S: eigen
Q: What are the eigenvalues of $A=\begin{pmatrix}3&1\\0&-2\end{pmatrix}$?
A: $3$ and $-2$
B: $3$ and $1$
C: $1$ and $-2$
D: $2$ and $-3$
ANS: A
EXP: For any triangular matrix the eigenvalues are the diagonal entries.
$\det(A-\lambda I)=(3-\lambda)(-2-\lambda)=0 \Rightarrow \lambda=3,\,-2$. The off-diagonal $1$ does not affect them.


@ la e la-e-003
T: Determinant of a scaled matrix
S: determinants
Q: $A$ is a $3\times3$ matrix with $\det A=4$. What is $\det(2A)$?
A: $8$
B: $16$
C: $32$
D: $64$
ANS: C
EXP: Scaling a matrix by $c$ scales every one of its $n$ rows by $c$, so $\det(cA)=c^n\det A$.
Here $\det(2A)=2^3\cdot 4=32$. A common trap is answering $2\cdot4=8$.


@ la e la-e-004
T: Determinant of the inverse
S: determinants
Q: A $3\times3$ matrix $A$ has eigenvalues $1,2,3$. What is $\det(A^{-1})$?
A: $6$
B: $\frac{1}{6}$
C: $-6$
D: $\frac{1}{2}$
ANS: B
EXP: $\det A$ is the product of eigenvalues: $1\cdot2\cdot3=6$.
Since $\det(A^{-1})=1/\det A$, the answer is $\frac16$. (Equivalently, $A^{-1}$ has eigenvalues $1,\tfrac12,\tfrac13$.)

@ la e la-e-007
T: Transpose of a product
S: matrix-ops
Q: For conformable matrices $A$ and $B$, $(AB)^T$ equals:
A: $A^TB^T$
B: $B^TA^T$
C: $BA$
D: $(BA)^T$
ANS: B
EXP: Transpose reverses the order: $(AB)^T=B^TA^T$.
Quick dimension check: if $A$ is $m\times n$ and $B$ is $n\times p$, then $(AB)^T$ is $p\times m$, and only $B^T(p\times n)\,A^T(n\times m)$ produces that shape.


@ la e la-e-008
T: Rank–nullity theorem
S: rank-nullity
Q: A $4\times6$ matrix has rank $3$. What is the dimension of its null space?
A: $1$
B: $2$
C: $3$
D: $6$
ANS: C
EXP: Rank–nullity: rank + nullity = number of columns $=6$.
So nullity $=6-3=3$. (Using rows instead, $4-3=1$, is the common mistake; that is the dimension of the left null space.)

S: vectors-norms
Q: What is the $L_2$ norm of $x=(3,4,12)$?
A: $19$
B: $13$
C: $12$

ANS: B

@ la e la-e-010
T: Eigenvalues of a real symmetric matrix
S: special-matrices
Q: The eigenvalues of every real symmetric matrix are:
A: always real
B: always positive
C: always distinct
D: always integers
ANS: A
EXP: The spectral theorem says a real symmetric matrix has real eigenvalues and an orthonormal set of eigenvectors.
They need not be positive (e.g. $\mathrm{diag}(1,-1)$) or distinct (e.g. $I$). This is why covariance matrices in PCA can always be diagonalised.

@ la m la-m-001
T: Eigenvalues of a 2×2 matrix
S: eigen
Q: What are the eigenvalues of $A=\begin{pmatrix}4&1\\2&3\end{pmatrix}$?
A: $5$ and $2$
B: $4$ and $3$
C: $6$ and $1$
D: $7$ and $10$
ANS: A
EXP: For a $2\times2$ matrix, $\lambda^2-(\operatorname{tr}A)\lambda+\det A=0$.
$\operatorname{tr}A=7$, $\det A=12-2=10$, so $\lambda^2-7\lambda+10=(\lambda-5)(\lambda-2)=0$.
Check: $5+2=7$ (trace) and $5\cdot2=10$ (det).


@ la m la-m-002
T: Eigenvalues of an idempotent matrix
S: special-matrices
Q: If $A^2=A$ (an idempotent / projection matrix), its eigenvalues can only be:
A: $0$ or $1$
B: $1$ or $-1$
C: any real number
D: $1$ only
ANS: A
EXP: If $Av=\lambda v$ with $v\ne0$, then $A^2v=\lambda^2v$. But $A^2v=Av=\lambda v$, so $\lambda^2=\lambda$, giving $\lambda\in\{0,1\}$.
Projection matrices such as the hat matrix $H=X(X^TX)^{-1}X^T$ in regression are idempotent.


@ la m la-m-003
T: Rank of the 1–9 matrix
S: rank-nullity
Q: What is the rank of $\begin{pmatrix}1&2&3\\4&5&6\\7&8&9\end{pmatrix}$?
A: $1$
B: $2$
C: $3$
D: $0$
ANS: B
EXP: $R_2-R_1=(3,3,3)$ and $R_3-R_2=(3,3,3)$, so $R_1-2R_2+R_3=0$: the rows are dependent and rank $<3$.
$R_1$ and $R_2$ are not multiples of each other, so rank $=2$ (and $\det=0$).


@ la m la-m-004
T: Properties of orthogonal matrices
S: special-matrices
Q: $Q$ is a real orthogonal matrix. Which statement is NOT necessarily true?
A: $Q^TQ=I$
B: $\|Qx\|=\|x\|$ for all $x$
C: $\det Q=\pm1$
D: $Q$ is symmetric
ANS: D
EXP: Orthogonality gives $Q^TQ=I$, which implies length preservation $\|Qx\|^2=x^TQ^TQx=\|x\|^2$ and $\det(Q)^2=1$.
It does not force symmetry: the rotation $\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ is orthogonal but $Q^T\ne Q$.

@ la m la-m-006

S: eigen
Q: $A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$. What are the eigenvalues of $B=A^3-2A+I$?
A: $0$ and $22$
B: $1$ and $3$
C: $0$ and $20$
D: $2$ and $22$
ANS: A
EXP: Eigenvalues of $A$: trace $4$, det $3$, so $\lambda=1,3$.

A: $\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}$

C: $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$
D: $\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$

@ la m la-m-008

Q: Let $x=(1,2,2)^T$. What is the only nonzero eigenvalue of $xx^T$?
A: $3$
B: $5$
C: $9$
D: $1$

@ la m la-m-010
T: Inverse via Cayley–Hamilton
S: eigen
Q: For $A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\3&4\end{pmatrix}$, which expression equals $A^{-1}$?
A: $\frac{1}{2}(A-5I)$
B: $\frac{1}{2}(5I-A)$
C: $\frac{1}{2}(A+5I)$
D: $\frac{1}{5}(A-2I)$
ANS: A
EXP: Characteristic polynomial: $\lambda^2-5\lambda-2$ (trace 5, det $-2$).
Cayley–Hamilton: $A^2-5A-2I=0\Rightarrow A(A-5I)=2I\Rightarrow A^{-1}=\tfrac12(A-5I)$.
Check: $\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}-4&2\\3&-1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-2&1\\1.5&-0.5\end{pmatrix}$, which is the inverse.


@ la h la-h-001
T: Determinant of I + all-ones matrix
S: determinants
Q: Let $J$ be the $4\times4$ matrix of all ones. What is $\det(I+J)$?
A: $1$
B: $4$
C: $5$
D: $16$
ANS: C
EXP: $J=\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^T$ has rank 1, with eigenvalue $\mathbf 1^T\mathbf 1=4$ (eigenvector $\mathbf 1$) and $0$ with multiplicity 3.
$I+J$ shifts every eigenvalue by 1: $5,1,1,1$. So $\det(I+J)=5$.
In general $\det(I+uv^T)=1+v^Tu$ (matrix determinant lemma).


@ la h la-h-002

A: the zero matrix
B: $A$

@ la h la-h-003
T: Least squares through the origin
S: projections-least-squares
Q: Fit $y=mx$ (no intercept) by least squares to the points $(1,2),(2,3),(3,7)$. What is $m$?
A: $\frac{29}{14}$
B: $2$
C: $\frac{7}{3}$
D: $\frac{29}{6}$
ANS: A
EXP: Minimise $\sum(y_i-mx_i)^2$. Setting the derivative to zero gives the normal equation $m=\dfrac{\sum x_iy_i}{\sum x_i^2}$.
$\sum x_iy_i=2+6+21=29$, $\sum x_i^2=1+4+9=14$, so $m=\tfrac{29}{14}\approx2.07$.
This is $(X^TX)^{-1}X^Ty$ with $X=(1,2,3)^T$. $\sum y/\sum x=2$ is a ratio of means, not the least squares fit.

@ la h la-h-006
T: Eckart–Young best low-rank approximation
S: svd-pca
Q: A matrix has singular values $5,3,1$. What is the Frobenius-norm error of its best rank-1 approximation?
A: $\sqrt{10}$
B: $3$
C: $4$
D: $\sqrt{34}$
ANS: A
EXP: Eckart–Young: the best rank-$k$ approximation keeps the top $k$ singular values (truncated SVD), and
$\|A-A_k\|_F=\sqrt{\sum_{i>k}\sigma_i^2}$. Here $\sqrt{3^2+1^2}=\sqrt{10}$.
In the spectral (2-) norm the error would be $\sigma_2=3$. This is the maths behind PCA and low-rank compression.


@ la h la-h-007
T: Convergence rate of power iteration
S: eigen
Q: $A$ has eigenvalues $4,-3,1$. Power iteration converges to the dominant eigenvector, with error shrinking each step by a factor of about:
A: $\frac14$
B: $\frac34$
C: $\frac13$
D: $\frac43$
ANS: B
EXP: Write $x_0=\sum c_iv_i$. Then $A^kx_0=4^k\left(c_1v_1+c_2(-\tfrac34)^kv_2+c_3(\tfrac14)^kv_3\right)$.
The slowest-decaying term is governed by $|\lambda_2/\lambda_1|=3/4$. Magnitudes matter, so $-3$ counts as 3.


@ la h la-h-008
T: What similar matrices share
S: eigen
Q: $B=P^{-1}AP$ for an invertible $P$. Which of these do $A$ and $B$ NOT necessarily share?
A: eigenvalues
B: determinant
C: rank
D: eigenvectors
ANS: D
EXP: Similar matrices represent the same linear map in different bases, so they share the characteristic polynomial (eigenvalues, trace, determinant) and rank.
Eigenvectors change with the basis: if $Av=\lambda v$ then $B(P^{-1}v)=\lambda(P^{-1}v)$, so $B$'s eigenvector is $P^{-1}v$.

@ la h la-h-010
T: Determinant of a Householder reflector
S: special-matrices
Q: For a nonzero $u\in\mathbb R^n$, let $H=I-\dfrac{2uu^T}{u^Tu}$. What is $\det H$?
A: $1$
B: $-1$
C: $0$
D: depends on $u$
ANS: B
EXP: $Hu=u-2u=-u$, so $u$ has eigenvalue $-1$.
Any $w\perp u$ gives $Hw=w$, so eigenvalue $1$ with multiplicity $n-1$.
$\det H=(-1)\cdot1^{n-1}=-1$. $H$ is a reflection: orthogonal and symmetric. It is used in QR decomposition.
