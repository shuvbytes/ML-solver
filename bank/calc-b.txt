S: single-var-optimization
TYPE: msq
Q: Newton's method $x_{k+1}=x_k-f'(x_k)/f''(x_k)$ is used to minimise the convex function $f(x)=\sqrt{1+x^2}$, whose minimiser is $0$. Which statements are true? (More than one may be correct.)
A: Starting at $x_0=0.5$, the iterates converge to $0$.
B: Starting at $x_0=2$, the first iterate is $x_1=-8$.
C: Because $f$ is convex, Newton's method converges from every starting point.
D: Starting at $x_0=1$, the iterates converge to $0$.
ANS: A,B
EXP: $f'(x)=\dfrac{x}{\sqrt{1+x^2}}$, $f''(x)=(1+x^2)^{-3/2}$, so $\dfrac{f'}{f''}=x(1+x^2)$ and the update is $x_{k+1}=x-x-x^3=-x^3$.

@ calc h calc-h-047
T: Minimisers of the sum of absolute deviations
S: single-var-optimization
Q: For the data $1,3,8,28$, which values of $c$ minimise $f(c)=\sum_{i=1}^4|x_i-c|$?
A: Only $c=5.5$
B: Only $c=10$
C: Every $c$ in $[3,8]$
D: Only $c=3$ and $c=8$
ANS: C
EXP: $f$ is piecewise linear. Away from the data points, $f'(c)=(\#\text{points below }c)-(\#\text{points above }c)$.
For $3<c<8$ there are 2 points below and 2 above, so $f'(c)=0$: $f$ is flat on $[3,8]$. For example $f(3)=2+0+5+25=32$ and $f(8)=7+5+0+20=32$.

$5.5$ is the usual "median" convention, a minimiser but not the only one. $10$ is the mean, which minimises squared error and is pulled toward the outlier $28$; $f(10)=36>32$. This robustness is why the L1 loss is used for outlier-heavy data.


@ calc h calc-h-048
T: Soft thresholding with an L1 penalty
S: single-var-optimization
Q: What is the minimiser of $g(x)=\frac12(x-1)^2+2|x|$ over all real $x$?
A: $-1$
B: $0$
C: $3$
D: $\frac15$
ANS: B
EXP: $|x|$ is not differentiable at $0$, so check each piece and the kink.
For $x>0$: $g'(x)=x-1+2=x+1>0$, so $g$ increases; no minimiser there.
For $x<0$: $g'(x)=x-1-2=x-3<0$, so $g$ decreases toward $0$; no minimiser there.
So the minimum is at the kink $x=0$, with $g(0)=\frac12$. In general the answer is the soft-thresholding rule $\operatorname{sign}(z)\max(|z|-\lambda,0)$ with $z=1$, $\lambda=2$, which gives $0$.
$-1$ comes from solving $x-1+2=0$ without checking that it lies in the region $x>0$ where that derivative holds. $\frac15$ treats the penalty as $2x^2$ (ridge). This exact zero is why the Lasso produces sparse weights.

@ calc m calc-m-053

S: integrals
TYPE: msq

Convex functions have no saddle points and no spurious local minima. This is why convex losses (linear/logistic regression, SVMs) are reliably solved by gradient methods, unlike neural networks.

@ calc e calc-e-047

S: constrained-optimization
