EXP: $b=a_1+a_2=A(1,1,0)^T$, so $x_p=(1,1,0)^T$ is a solution and the system is consistent.
Since rank $=2$, the null space has dimension $3-2=1$, and every $x_p+t\,n$ (with $n$ spanning the null space) is also a solution: infinitely many.
[[C]] is the trap: a singular $A$ means $Ax=b$ has either no solution or infinitely many, never exactly one; which case occurs depends on whether $b$ is in the column space. A linear system never has exactly two solutions.

@ la m la-m-067
T: Rank of the centring matrix
S: rank-nullity
Q: Let $H=I_5-\frac15\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^T$, the $5\times5$ matrix that subtracts the mean from a vector ($Hx=x-\bar x\mathbf 1$). What is $\operatorname{rank}H$?
A: $4$
B: $5$
C: $1$
D: $0$
ANS: A
EXP: $Hx=0\iff x=\bar x\mathbf 1$, i.e. $x$ is a constant vector. So $\operatorname{null}(H)=\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf 1\}$ has dimension $1$.
Rank–nullity: $\operatorname{rank}H=5-1=4$.
Also $H$ is a projection ($H^2=H$), so $\operatorname{rank}H=\operatorname{tr}H=5\left(1-\tfrac15\right)=4$.

@ la m la-m-070
T: Sparsity of the LU factors of a tridiagonal matrix
S: lu
Q: $A$ is an $n\times n$ tridiagonal matrix (nonzeros only on the main diagonal and the two adjacent diagonals) for which elimination needs no row exchanges. What structure do the factors in $A=LU$ have?
A: $L$ is lower bidiagonal and $U$ is upper bidiagonal
B: $L$ and $U$ are full lower and upper triangular matrices
C: $L$ is the identity and $U=A$
D: $L$ is lower bidiagonal and $U$ is full upper triangular
ANS: A
EXP: When eliminating below pivot $k$, only row $k+1$ has a nonzero in column $k$, so only one multiplier $\ell_{k+1,k}$ is needed. Subtracting a multiple of row $k$ (nonzeros in columns $k,k+1$) from row $k+1$ creates no new nonzeros.
So $L$ has only its diagonal and first subdiagonal, and $U$ only its diagonal and first superdiagonal. Example: $\begin{pmatrix}2&1&0\\1&2&1\\0&1&2\end{pmatrix}$ gives $\ell_{21}=\frac12$, $\ell_{32}=\frac23$ and $U$ with diagonal $2,\frac32,\frac43$ and superdiagonal $1,1$.
There is no fill-in, so the factorisation costs $O(n)$ instead of $O(n^3)$ (the Thomas algorithm, used for splines and 1-D PDEs).

@ la m la-m-071
T: Consistency of an overdetermined system
S: linear-systems
Q: For which $k$ does the system $x+y=3,\ x-y=1,\ 2x+3y=k$ have a solution?
A: $k=7$
B: $k=8$
C: Every real $k$
D: No value of $k$; three equations in two unknowns are never consistent
ANS: A
EXP: The first two equations already fix the unknowns: adding gives $2x=4$, so $x=2$, $y=1$.
The third equation must agree: $2(2)+3(1)=7$, so the system is consistent only for $k=7$ (and then the solution is unique).
In rank terms: $\operatorname{rank}A=2$, and $\operatorname{rank}[A\mid b]=2$ only when $b$ lies in the column space, which here means $k=7$.
[[D]] is the trap: more equations than unknowns usually means no solution, but not always. When it fails, least squares gives the best approximate solution.

@ la m la-m-075
T: Largest singular value of the pseudo-inverse
S: svd-pca
Q: A $3\times3$ matrix $A$ has singular values $5$, $2$ and $0$. What is the largest singular value of its Moore–Penrose pseudo-inverse $A^+$?
A: $\frac12$
B: $\frac15$
C: $5$
D: $A^+$ does not exist because $A$ is singular
ANS: A
EXP: If $A=U\Sigma V^T$, then $A^+=V\Sigma^+U^T$, where $\Sigma^+$ inverts the nonzero singular values and keeps the zeros: $\frac15,\ \frac12,\ 0$.
The largest is $\frac12$, coming from the smallest nonzero singular value of $A$.
[[B]] is the trap of inverting the largest one. [[D]] is wrong: the pseudo-inverse exists for every matrix, singular or rectangular. This is why tiny nonzero singular values make least-squares solutions $x=A^+b$ unstable, and why ridge regression or truncated SVD is used to tame them.
